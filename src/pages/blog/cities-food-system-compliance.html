For city and municipal leaders

Cities as food system actors

Why digital redistribution infrastructure is now a municipal compliance tool.

Ninja Fedy

19 August 2026 · 7 min read

For most of the past decade, municipal food waste initiatives have been framed as sustainability ambitions. Progressive cities set targets, funded pilots, published strategies. The targets were meaningful but not binding. The reporting was voluntary. The accountability was reputational, not legal.

That framing has changed.

The regulatory shift

The September 2025 amendment to the EU Waste Framework Directive established binding food waste reduction targets for all 27 EU Member States. By 31 December 2030, member states must achieve a 10% reduction at processing and manufacturing stage, and a 30% per capita reduction at distribution and consumption stages. Finland designated its competent national authority by the January 2026 deadline.

Why data is the central challenge

The target is a 30% reduction. Demonstrating that reduction requires a baseline, a measurement methodology, and a data collection system that produces verifiable numbers. Most Finnish municipalities do not yet have all three. Food waste data at municipal level is currently fragmented, collected through varying methods, reaching city sustainability offices through intermediaries with inconsistent formats.

Helsinki: building the evidence base

Helsinki’s Testbed programme funded a 21-day pilot with Stadin Safka. The operational results from the pilot: coordinators estimated saving around 34 hours a month, real-time donor visibility, fewer wasted pickup trips. Every donation logged through Zipli is a timestamped, verifiable entry. The hub’s annual impact report, previously assembled manually, now generates automatically.

Turku: moving from pilot to procurement

Turku’s Bloomberg Mayors Challenge win resulted in a market consultation (tietopyyntö) that described the operational requirements with a level of precision that reflects months of thinking about exactly how this infrastructure needs to work. It asked suppliers to propose solutions for coordinating incoming donations at a logistics hub, tracking food flows from donor to redistribution to recipient, and reporting impact in a format usable by city officials. That is Zipli’s product description.

The replicable model

What Helsinki and Turku have demonstrated is a replicable model: cities can use digital redistribution infrastructure to pursue food waste targets, fund hub operations, and build EU compliance evidence simultaneously. The same data that makes a hub more efficient is also the data that a city sustainability office needs for its reporting.

For Finnish municipalities currently reviewing their food waste strategies, the question is not whether digital coordination tools are worth investing in. The regulatory obligation, the evidence base from Helsinki and Turku, and the 2030 deadline make the case clearly.
